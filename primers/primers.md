# Biofuels and Vegetable Oils Desk: Variable Primers

Plain-language, desk-level primers on every variable we track. Market levels quoted are late-November 2026 unless stated. Policy is as of 25 November 2026.

---

## 1. Soybean oil (CBOT ZL)

**What it is.** CBOT soybean oil futures price crude degummed soybean oil in cents per pound (60,000 lb contract, delivered by warehouse receipt in the Midwest). It is the single largest US biofuel feedstock and the marginal price-setter for the whole US fats-and-oils complex. More than half of US soybean oil now goes to biodiesel, renewable diesel and SAF.

**How it moves prices.** ZL sits between two worlds: the oilseed balance sheet (crush pace, stocks, exports, palm and canola) and the energy/credit stack (ULSD, D4 RINs, LCFS, 45Z). Higher RVOs, higher D4 RINs, higher ULSD or a richer 45Z all raise what a biofuel plant can pay for oil, which is bullish. Record crush, falling distillate cracks, SRE waivers or weak policy news are bearish. Policy headlines move ZL more than weather.

**What to watch.** EPA RFS rulemakings and SRE decisions; Treasury 45Z guidance; monthly EMTS RIN data; NOPA crush (around the 15th); USDA Fats & Oils; WASDE (biofuel use line); EIA feedstock data; NYMEX HO; Bursa palm.

**Rule of thumb.** 1¢/lb on ZL ≈ 7.5¢/gal of biodiesel feedstock cost. A 10¢/gal move in D4 is worth about 15¢/gal to a biodiesel plant, or about 2¢/lb of oil. In 2026 ZL ranged from 48¢ (January) to 80¢ (June) and sat near 60¢ in late November.

## 2. Soybean crush and oil share

**What it is.** Crushing a 60-lb bushel yields about 11.7–11.9 lb of oil and about 44 lb of 48%-protein meal, plus hulls. The board crush margin = meal ($/short ton × 0.022) + oil (¢/lb × 0.11) − soybeans ($/bu). Oil share = oil value ÷ (oil + meal value).

**How it moves prices.** Biofuel demand turned soybeans into an oil-led crush. When oil share is high (above 45–50%), oil is the reason plants run, and meal becomes the by-product that must clear at whatever price feed buyers pay. New crush plants raise oil supply and depress meal. A policy shock therefore hits oil share first: bullish RFS news widens oil share; bearish news compresses it. The crush itself is capped by meal demand and export capacity, so ever-higher oil demand ultimately needs either higher oil prices or more imported feedstock.

**What to watch.** NOPA monthly crush and oil stocks; USDA Fats & Oils; new-plant start-ups; meal export sales; Argentine export taxes (they set the world meal price).

**Rule of thumb.** Each 100 M bu of extra crush adds about 1.18 B lb of oil, enough for roughly 155 M gal of biodiesel. US crush runs about 2.8 B bu in 2026/27. NOPA oil stocks near 1.05 B lb (September 2026) were the tightest since 2014.

## 3. Palm oil (Bursa FCPO) and the POGO / palm–soy spread

**What it is.** Bursa Malaysia crude palm oil futures (FCPO, ringgit per tonne) price the world's largest-volume vegetable oil. Indonesia and Malaysia produce about 85% of supply. POGO is palm oil minus ICE gasoil ($/t), and the soy–palm spread (BOPO) is soybean oil minus palm oil.

**How it moves prices.** Palm is the world's cheap-oil swing supply. Indonesia's biodiesel mandate (B40 to B50 from July 2026) removes exportable palm, which is funded by the export levy (raised to 12.5% for CPO in March 2026). When POGO is negative, palm biodiesel pays its way on energy alone and Asian blending is easy. When POGO is strongly positive, mandates are subsidy-hungry. Palm cannot earn US RINs at scale or California LCFS credits, so it pulls on soy through food demand (India, China): a wide BOPO pushes importers from soy to palm and weakens world soy oil.

**What to watch.** MPOB monthly data (around the 10th); Indonesian GAPKI data; export levy and reference price; B50 rollout; cargo surveyors (ITS, AmSpec); El Niño and seasonal output.

**Rule of thumb.** A BOPO above $150–200/t usually shifts Indian buying to palm. Each 10 points of Indonesian blend (B40 to B50) absorbs about 2–3 Mt of CPO a year. Late November 2026: Dec FCPO about 4,190 MYR/t, POGO about −$80/t, BOPO about +$230/t.

## 4. Canola / rapeseed oil

**What it is.** Canola oil is crushed mainly in Canada (Prairies) and rapeseed oil in the EU. It is the main non-soy crop oil in US renewable diesel and is priced off ICE canola (Winnipeg), Euronext rapeseed and Dutch FOB rapeseed oil.

**How it moves prices.** In the US, canola competes directly with soybean oil for RD and SAF slots. Its lower lifecycle CI (smaller ILUC penalty) helps under LCFS, while 45Z eligibility depends on North American origin. The China–Canada dispute matters: China cut seed duties (5.9% anti-dumping) and suspended its meal tariff through 2028, but kept 100% on Canadian canola oil. That pushes Canadian oil south into US biofuel plants and caps US oil prices. The US half-RIN rule for foreign feedstock, deferred to 2028+, is the key long-dated risk for imported canola oil.

**What to watch.** ICE canola; Statistics Canada crush and stocks; US imports of canola oil (Census); China tariff decisions; Canada CFR changes; EU RED III crop caps.

**Rule of thumb.** Canola oil usually trades at a premium of a few ¢/lb to soybean oil in US delivered terms. July 2026 delivered feedstock was about 631¢/gal, against about 566¢/gal for soybean oil.

## 5. Used cooking oil (UCO)

**What it is.** UCO is collected restaurant and food-processing frying oil, reported in US data within "yellow grease." It is a waste feedstock with a low CI score (typically about 15–30 gCO2e/MJ), prized for California LCFS and SAF.

**How it moves prices.** UCO's value is mostly credit value: under LCFS a UCO gallon earns roughly twice the credits of a soy gallon. When credit prices rise, UCO's premium over soybean oil widens. Since 2026, 45Z pays only for North American feedstock, and China's export tax rebate ended in December 2024. US imports of Chinese UCO fell sharply (China-origin shipments to the US were down about 60–70% y/y in 2026), and Chinese material was redirected to Europe and Singapore for SAF. Domestic UCO collected in the US, Canada or Mexico keeps full 45Z eligibility.

**What to watch.** EIA feedstock data (yellow grease); Census imports by origin; ARA and Asian UCO prices; EU SAF demand; 45Z rules and the 2028 half-RIN rule; fraud and traceability actions.

**Feedstock spreads.** Waste fats are quoted as a spread to front-month soybean oil and follow the board with a lag of a week or two, so two or three weeks of falling soybean oil usually show up next in UCO, yellow grease and tallow bids. In late November 2026 Midwest UCO sat about 2¢/lb over soybean oil, yellow grease about 3.5¢ under, and bleachable tallow about 4¢ over UCO (industry estimates). A narrowing discount of yellow grease to soybean oil means renderers have pricing power; a widening one means feedstock is in surplus.

**Rule of thumb.** US Gulf delivered UCO was about 68.5¢/lb against about 60¢ for soybean oil (November 2026). Under 2026 45Z, UCO's CI advantage is worth about 5¢/lb over soy, and under LCFS it is worth much more at $80–90 credits.

## 6. Tallow and other animal fats

**What it is.** Tallow (beef fat), choice white grease (pork), poultry fat and fish oils are rendered by-products of meat production. They are low-CI feedstocks (typically about 30–45 gCO2e/MJ for RD) and a staple of large RD plants with pretreatment units.

**How it moves prices.** Supply is fixed by slaughter rates, not by price, so higher biofuel demand shows up almost entirely as higher fat prices and imports. Animal fats now price like an energy product, tracking soybean oil plus their CI premium. Imports have shifted origin: Brazilian tallow fell while Australian, British and Irish tallow rose in 2026. Imported fat lost 45Z eligibility from 2026, which favors domestic fat and lifts its price.

**What to watch.** USDA cattle and hog slaughter; Jacobsen and USDA tallow quotes; EIA feedstock (tallow, white grease, poultry); Census imports (Brazil, Australia); tariffs on Brazil; Fastmarkets/Argus fat-to-soy spreads.

**Rule of thumb.** Supply follows slaughter with about a one-month lag: a heavy month of cattle slaughter shows up as more tallow in the next USDA Fats and Oils report. Bleachable tallow traded about 6¢/lb over soybean oil in November 2026 (industry estimates), so the old rule that tallow sits at a discount no longer holds while credits are high. Historically, tallow traded at a few ¢/lb discount to soybean oil, narrowing when credit prices rise. Tallow is about 20% of US biofuel feedstock today and is projected to reach about 27% by 2027 (industry estimates).

## 7. Distillers corn oil (DCO)

**What it is.** DCO is oil recovered from ethanol plant stillage, about 0.8–1.0 lb per bushel of corn ground. It is inedible, low-CI (about 20–35 gCO2e/MJ) and fully domestic.

**How it moves prices.** DCO supply is tied to ethanol output, so it grows only with corn grind and extraction technology, not with biofuel demand. It trades as a discount or premium to soybean oil depending on credit value: higher LCFS and 45Z values lift DCO toward or above soy. Because it is domestic and non-crop, DCO escapes the California 20% crop cap and the import RIN haircut. It is one of the feedstocks that most benefits from current policy design.

**What to watch.** EIA weekly ethanol production; EIA feedstock data (corn oil); ethanol margins; USDA Grain Crushings report.

**Rule of thumb.** US ethanol at about 1.1 M b/d supports roughly 4.5–5.5 B lb of DCO a year. The 2026 45Z CI premium for DCO was about 4.7¢/lb over soy.

## 8. Biodiesel (FAME)

**What it is.** Biodiesel is fatty acid methyl ester made by transesterifying fats and oils with methanol. It yields about 1 gal of fuel per 7.5–7.6 lb of feedstock, with glycerin as a by-product. It is blended into diesel (B5–B20), limited by cold flow and engine warranties. It earns 1.5 D4 RINs per gallon.

**How it moves prices.** Biodiesel is the most price-elastic BBD supply: dozens of small and mid plants switch on and off with the margin. That makes FAME the **marginal gallon**, so D4 RIN prices often settle where a soy biodiesel plant just breaks even. Its RIN value per gallon (1.5 EV) is now equal to RD's from 2027, when RD drops to 1.5, which improves FAME's relative economics. It cannot be used at high blends, so the blend wall and winter demand matter.

**What to watch.** EMTS D4 generation (biodiesel line); EIA monthly production; NYMEX HO; the BOHO spread; physical B100 differentials to ULSD; plant restarts and closures.

**Rule of thumb.** US biodiesel capacity is about 2.0 B gal/yr (128 kb/d), running at about 85–90% in late 2026. Physical B100 trades at a differential to NY Harbor ULSD futures, which ran −$0.95 to −$1.40/gal on some forward Midwest sales in October 2026.

## 9. Renewable diesel (HVO)

**What it is.** Renewable diesel is hydrotreated vegetable oil: fats and oils reacted with hydrogen to make paraffinic diesel chemically equivalent to petroleum diesel. It is a drop-in fuel usable at 100%. It needs about 8.1–8.5 lb of feedstock per gallon and yields naphtha and propane by-products. It earns 1.7 (some 1.6) D4 RINs per gallon through 2026, then 1.5 from 2027.

**How it moves prices.** RD capacity (about 5 B gal/yr including other biofuels) is far larger than biodiesel's and concentrated in large refinery-integrated plants. Those plants pay up for low-CI waste fats to maximise California LCFS credits. When LCFS prices are weak, RD plants cut runs or swing to SAF. The 2027 EV cut to 1.5 removes about 0.2 RINs (about 40¢/gal at a $2 D4), so RD margins fall relative to FAME.

**What to watch.** EMTS D4 (RD line); EIA STEO RD forecast (cut for 2027 in November 2026); LCFS credit price; refinery earnings calls; plant conversions to SAF; RD exports to Europe and Canada.

**Rule of thumb.** Each $10/t on the LCFS credit is worth about 2–3¢/gal for soy RD and about 7–8¢/gal for UCO RD. RD ran at about 72% of capacity in September 2026.

## 10. Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF / HEFA)

**What it is.** HEFA-SPK jet fuel is made by the same hydrotreating route as RD, with extra isomerisation and cracking, from the same fats and oils. It can be blended up to 50% with fossil jet. It earns D4 RINs (1.5 from 2027), state credits, and the same 45Z rate as other fuels from 2026. The old SAF premium (up to $1.75/gal) ended for fuel produced after 2025.

**How it moves prices.** US SAF demand is mostly voluntary plus credits. EU ReFuelEU (2% in 2025, 6% in 2030) and the UK mandates create the hard demand. SAF yields less jet per lb than RD yields diesel, so it is feedstock-intensive and pulls hardest on waste oils (UCO, tallow). The loss of the US SAF premium after 2025 lowered the incentive to make jet rather than RD in the US. Europe absorbs Chinese UCO and HEFA, which kept EU SAF premiums at record lows in late 2026.

**What to watch.** EMTS renewable jet RIN generation; EU and UK SAF mandates and penalties; Argus/Platts SAF premiums; RD-to-SAF conversions; airline offtake deals.

**Rule of thumb.** US renewable jet was only about 20–30 M gal a month in 2026, versus about 300 M gal of RD. It is small but growing, and every SAF gallon is about 1.1 gal of RD feedstock demand.

## 11. Ethanol (link only)

**What it is.** Corn ethanol (about 2.9 gal per bushel) earns D6 RINs and fills the 15 B gal implied conventional mandate. It links to the oils desk through D6 RINs, the RIN nesting, and DCO supply.

**How it moves prices.** The fixed 15 B gal conventional bucket is usually met by ethanol blended at about 10% (E10), so D6 trades on the E10 blend wall, E15 access and the size of the gasoline pool. A smaller-than-EPA-assumed gasoline pool in 2026 left D6 supply comfortable relative to the requirement, pulling D6 below D4. When D6 nears D4, extra biodiesel can count toward the conventional bucket, and the nesting starts to matter for oils.

**What to watch.** EIA weekly ethanol output and stocks; E15 legislation; D6 RIN; EIA gasoline demand.

**Rule of thumb.** The D4–D6 spread was about 10–12¢ in late October 2026. A spread near zero means biodiesel is filling ethanol's gap, which is bullish for soybean oil.

## 12. Heating oil / ULSD (NY Harbor HO)

**What it is.** NYMEX NY Harbor ULSD futures (HO, $/gal) are the benchmark for US distillate. Biodiesel and RD are priced and sold as a differential to HO.

**How it moves prices.** HO is the energy floor of a biofuel gallon's revenue. Higher HO lifts biofuel margins and so raises what plants pay for oil, which is bullish for ZL. Lower HO squeezes margins, so RIN prices must rise to keep plants running. Because the RVO is fixed, the RIN does most of the adjusting: HO and D4 tend to move in **opposite** directions over time, offsetting each other. That is why the sum (HO + RIN × EV), not HO alone, drives oil demand. 2026's Middle East supply shock took HO from about $2.05 to $4.61/gal before it settled near $3.90–4.00 in November.

**What to watch.** EIA weekly distillate stocks; refinery outages; crude (WTI, Brent); diesel cracks; ICE gasoil; winter weather.

**Rule of thumb.** For FAME, $0.10/gal on HO ≈ 1.3¢/lb of oil buying power. The HO crack was about $83/bbl in mid-November 2026.

---

## 13. The BOHO spread

**What it is.** BOHO = soybean oil (¢/lb) × 7.5 ÷ 100 − HO ($/gal), quoted in ¢/gal. It is the feedstock cost of a gallon of biodiesel over the diesel it replaces: the gap the credit stack (RINs, LCFS, 45Z) must close.

**How it moves prices.** A wide BOHO means biofuel needs more subsidy, so D4 RINs must be higher to keep the marginal plant running. A narrow or negative BOHO means biofuel is nearly economic on energy alone and RINs can fall. Traders watch BOHO against the D4 RIN × 1.5: when RIN value per gallon greatly exceeds BOHO plus processing cost, margins are fat and production rises, which eventually pressures RINs. BOHO is volatile because it combines two volatile legs.

**What to watch.** ZL and HO settlements; the forward BOHO curve (seasonality, spreads); D4 RIN against BOHO.

**Rule of thumb.** Breakeven D4 ≈ (BOHO + opex − 45Z − LCFS − glycerin) ÷ 1.5, adjusted for the physical B100 discount to HO. Dec BOHO was about +56¢/gal on 18 November 2026 (ZL 60.0¢, HO $3.94), against a D4 of about $2.00.

## 14. RINs in general: generation, separation, retirement

**What it is.** A Renewable Identification Number is the RFS compliance credit. It is generated in EPA's EMTS when a gallon of qualifying fuel is produced or imported: one RIN per ethanol-equivalent gallon, so a gallon of biodiesel generates 1.5. It is attached to the fuel until the fuel is blended into transportation fuel or exported, then **separated** and tradeable. Obligated parties (refiners and importers) **retire** RINs to meet their RVO, and each RIN carries a vintage year.

**How it moves prices.** RIN prices are the market's price of the gap between mandated and economic blending. A tight mandate relative to supply means the price must rise until the marginal gallon is produced. Carryover RINs from prior years (up to 20% of an obligation can come from the previous vintage) are a buffer. A large carryover bank caps prices, and a depleted bank makes prices spike. SREs remove demand. Fixed percentage standards mean a smaller fuel pool lowers RIN demand.

**What to watch.** EPA EMTS monthly generation and separation; EPA weekly RIN prices; carryover bank estimates; RVO and SRE news; compliance deadlines.

**Rule of thumb.** The RIN value per gallon of biodiesel is the D4 price × 1.5. At $2.00 that is $3.00/gal, the largest single revenue item after the fuel itself.

## 15. D4 RIN (biomass-based diesel)

**What it is.** The D4 RIN is the credit generated by biodiesel, renewable diesel, renewable jet and heating oil from qualifying fats and oils. It satisfies the BBD mandate and, through the nesting, also counts toward advanced and total.

**How it moves prices.** D4 is the direct link between EPA policy and vegetable oils. The final Set 2 rule set BBD at 9.07 B (2026) and 9.20 B (2027) RINs, about 70% above 2025, which drove D4 from about $1 to a peak near $2.41 (June 2026). It eased back to about $2.00 by November as strong production, lower HO-adjusted margins, a pending SRE reallocation and a smaller diesel pool weighed. The 2027 EV cut (RD to 1.5) and the 2028 half-RIN rule are forward drivers.

**What to watch.** EMTS D4 generation against the requirement (about 63% of the 2026 need was met through September); SRE reallocation final rule; 45Z final regulations; D4 futures curve (Dec-26 against Dec-27 vintage); ZL and HO.

**Rule of thumb.** About 1.05 B D4 RINs a month were needed in October–December 2026 to cover 2026 from current output, against about 0.79 B generated in September. The gap is covered by carryover and imports. Each 10¢ on D4 ≈ 2¢/lb of soybean oil value.

## 16. D5 RIN (advanced biofuel)

**What it is.** The D5 RIN comes from non-cellulosic advanced fuels other than BBD: imported sugarcane ethanol, renewable naphtha, some biogas and other advanced pathways. It satisfies the advanced (non-BBD) slice.

**How it moves prices.** EPA's advanced mandate exceeds BBD plus cellulosic by only about 0.67 B RINs (2026). That gap is filled either by D5 fuels or by extra D4. Because D4 is the swing supply, D5 usually trades at or just below D4 when the advanced gap binds, and near D6 when it does not. A D5–D6 spread that opens up is a sign the advanced bucket is tight.

**What to watch.** EMTS D5 generation; Brazilian ethanol imports; naphtha RIN output; the advanced minus BBD minus cellulosic gap in each RVO.

**Rule of thumb.** If D5 trades near D4, extra biodiesel is setting the advanced price. If D5 is near D6, the advanced gap is filled cheaply and has no extra pull on oils.

---

## 17. D6 RIN (conventional renewable fuel)

**What it is.** The D6 RIN comes mainly from corn-starch ethanol, plus older grandfathered biodiesel capacity. It fills the conventional bucket (total minus advanced, an implied 15 B gal).

**How it moves prices.** D6 prices the ethanol blend wall. When ethanol demand easily covers 15 B, D6 trades at a discount to D4. When it doesn't, any RIN can fill the conventional gap, D4 gallons are pulled in, and D6 converges to D4. In 2026 D6 rallied to record levels above $2 with D4 in the spring, then fell relative to D4 in October, to about 165–180¢, as ethanol output and stocks rose and EIA's gasoline pool forecast fell below EPA's assumption.

**What to watch.** EIA weekly ethanol; E15 policy; gasoline demand; D6 supply against requirement (about 73% through September 2026).

**Rule of thumb.** Watch the D4–D6 spread. Near zero means biodiesel is filling the conventional bucket (bullish oils). Above 30–50¢ means separate markets.

---

## 18. D3 RIN (cellulosic)

**What it is.** The D3 RIN is the cellulosic biofuel credit, generated today almost entirely by renewable natural gas (landfill and digester gas) used as vehicle CNG/LNG, plus corn kernel fiber ethanol.

**How it moves prices.** D3 is a separate, RNG-driven market. EPA sets the cellulosic volume close to expected production, at 1.36 B (2026) and 1.43 B (2027), and waived part of 2025. Cellulosic waiver credits are available for 2025 but not for 2026–27, which removes the old price ceiling. D3 sits at the top of the nesting and can satisfy advanced and total, so it never trades below D5 and D6 in a functioning market. eRINs (electricity) were removed in 2026.

**What to watch.** EMTS D3 generation; RNG project start-ups; EPA cellulosic waivers; 45Z manure pathways.

**Rule of thumb.** D3 matters to oils only through the nesting: a shortfall in D3 is not filled by D4. It has little direct link to soybean oil.

## 19. The RIN nesting (D3 / D4 / D5 / D6 hierarchy)

**What it is.** RFS categories are nested. Cellulosic (D3) and BBD (D4) are inside advanced; advanced is inside total. A higher-tier RIN can be used for a lower-tier obligation but not the reverse. D3 and D4 RINs count toward advanced and total, and D5 counts toward advanced and total. D6 counts only toward total.

**How it moves prices.** Prices sort themselves: D3 ≥ D4 ≥ D5 ≥ D6 in normal markets. If the BBD mandate is binding and the advanced and conventional gaps are filled by cheaper fuels, D4 trades at a premium and D5 and D6 trade lower. If ethanol can't fill the conventional bucket, D6 is pulled up to D4. **A narrow D4–D6 spread means biodiesel is the marginal gallon for the whole RFS**: oil demand is set by the total mandate, not just BBD. A wide spread means oils depend only on the BBD number.

**What to watch.** D4–D6 and D4–D5 spreads; implied conventional (total minus advanced); advanced minus BBD minus cellulosic gap.

**Rule of thumb.** In 2026 the D4–D6 spread ranged from near zero (spring) to about 12¢ (late October). Every billion gallons of conventional shortfall filled by biodiesel is about 0.67 B gal of BD, or about 5 B lb of oil.

## 20. RVO (renewable volume obligation) and mandate-to-feedstock conversion

**What it is.** EPA sets annual volumes (RINs) for cellulosic, BBD, advanced and total. It converts them into percentage standards by dividing by projected non-exempt gasoline and diesel. Each obligated party's RVO = its fuel sales × percentage. Set 2 (final 27 March 2026) is the first to set every category in RINs.

**How it moves prices.** The RVO is the demand curve for D4 and, by extension, for fats and oils. Converting it: physical gallons = RINs ÷ EV (about 1.6 for the BD/RD mix, 1.5 from 2027); feedstock = gallons × 7.5–8.5 lb. Fixed percentages mean that if the fuel pool shrinks, actual RIN demand falls. EIA's late-2026 view of a pool about 3.7 B gal smaller than EPA assumed is bearish at the margin.

**What to watch.** RFS proposals and finals (Set 3 for 2028+); SRE reallocations; EIA STEO fuel demand; carryover RIN bank.

**Rule of thumb.** 2026 BBD of 9.07 B RINs ÷ 1.6 ≈ 5.7 B gal × ~8 lb ≈ **45 B lb** of fats and oils, before carryover and imports. USDA has 17.8 B lb of that coming from soybean oil in 2026/27.

## 21. Small refinery exemptions (SREs) and reallocation

**What it is.** Refineries under 75,000 b/d can petition EPA for relief from their RFS obligation for "disproportionate economic hardship." Exempted volumes reduce RIN demand unless EPA **reallocates** them to other obligated parties.

**How it moves prices.** SRE grants without reallocation are bearish for RINs and soybean oil, because they cut demand and release returned RINs. Reallocation restores demand. In August 2025 EPA cleared a backlog of 175 petitions (about 5.3 B RINs exempted for 2016–2024). Set 2 then reallocated 70% of 2023–25 volumes into 2026–27. For 2025, EPA exempted 1.76 B RINs (August 2026), about 0.77 B more than projected, and in October 2026 proposed reallocating 100% of the overshoot (comments close 4 December 2026).

**What to watch.** EPA SRE decision notices; the final reallocation rule; DOE hardship methodology review; litigation; Supreme Court and appeals court precedents.

**Rule of thumb.** 1 B RINs of unreallocated SREs ≈ 0.6 B gal of BBD ≈ 5 B lb of oil demand lost. The market trades SRE news as a binary on reallocation, so each headline can move D4 by 10–20¢.

## 22. The blenders' tax credit (old BTC) vs 45Z

**What it is.** The old $1.00/gal biodiesel mixture credit (40A/6426) went to the **blender**, applied to imported fuel and any feedstock, and was flat. It expired 31 December 2024. 45Z, from 2025, goes to the **producer**, applies only to US production, scales with carbon intensity and, from 2026, requires North American feedstock.

**How it moves prices.** The BTC was effectively a $1 subsidy shared along the chain, which lowered the RIN needed to clear the mandate. 45Z pays a soy biodiesel gallon only about $0.45–0.50, so the RIN must do more of the work. Industry estimates of the D4 needed at a common BOHO run from about $0.69 under the BTC to about $1.56 under 45Z as amended. 45Z also shut out imported fuel and foreign feedstock, which shifted supply toward US crush and domestic fats.

**What to watch.** 45Z final regulations; any legislation to revive a blender credit; import flows of BD and RD.

**Rule of thumb.** Moving from BTC to 45Z took about 50¢/gal of subsidy off soy biodiesel, equivalent to about 33¢ of D4 at a 1.5 EV.

## 23. 45Z clean fuel production credit

**What it is.** 45Z is the federal producer tax credit for low-carbon transportation fuel, for fuel produced in 2025 through 2029 (extended by the July 2025 reconciliation law). Credit/gal = $1.00 × (50 − CI) ÷ 50, where CI is in kg CO2e/mmBtu from the 45ZCF-GREET model, inflation-adjusted. It is $0.20 maximum without prevailing-wage compliance. It cannot go below zero, except for manure RNG. From 2026: no ILUC penalty, North American feedstock only, and no separate SAF premium.

**How it moves prices.** 45Z adds credit value per gallon in proportion to how far below 50 a fuel scores. Removing ILUC dropped soybean oil's score to about 27, worth about 49¢/gal in 2026, and narrowed the waste-oil premium over soy to about 4–5¢/lb. That is bullish for soybean oil and canola relative to imported UCO and tallow. Climate-smart farm practice credits (USDA FD-CIC) can lower crop-oil CI further.

**What to watch.** Final Treasury regulations (targeted November 2026, pending); annual emissions rate tables (Notice 2026-53); GREET updates; inflation factor; credit transfer prices.

**Rule of thumb.** Each 1 kg/mmBtu of CI is worth 2¢/gal. Soy biodiesel at CI 27 earns ≈ $0.46 before inflation adjustment; tallow and UCO score lower and earn more.

## 24. Carbon intensity (CI) scores

**What it is.** CI is a fuel's lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions per unit of energy: farm or collection, transport, processing and combustion, plus land-use change where included. California LCFS uses gCO2e/MJ (CA-GREET); 45Z uses kg CO2e/mmBtu (45ZCF-GREET, multiply g/MJ by 1.055). Each plant and feedstock combination has a certified pathway score.

**How it moves prices.** CI turns a feedstock's origin into dollars. Lower CI means more LCFS and 45Z credit per gallon, so low-CI feedstocks (UCO, tallow, DCO) earn premiums over crop oils that widen when credit prices rise. California's ILUC values (soybean about 29, canola about 14.5, palm about 71 gCO2e/MJ) are a large part of crop-oil CI. Removing ILUC under 45Z was a structural win for soy.

**What to watch.** CARB pathway certifications; GREET model updates; 45Z emissions tables; CARB crop-cap and sustainability certification (2028).

**Rule of thumb.** Typical LCFS CIs (gCO2e/MJ): soy BD/RD about 50–60, canola about 45–55, DCO about 20–35, tallow about 30–45, UCO about 15–30. The 2026 California diesel standard is about 80.2.

## 25. California LCFS credit

**What it is.** One LCFS credit = 1 metric ton of CO2e reduced below the annual CI standard. Fuels below the standard generate credits; fossil fuels above it generate deficits. Credits trade bilaterally and are reported monthly by CARB.

**How it moves prices.** The credit price sets how much California pays for low-CI diesel, so it governs RD and BD flows into the state and the premium for waste fats. The July 2025 amendments tightened standards (a one-time cut to 22.75% in 2025, 30% by 2030, 90% by 2045). They also added an Automatic Acceleration Mechanism (earliest trigger May 2027) and a 20% cap on crop-oil credits from 2028. As deficits outran credits in 2026, prices rose from about $57 (Q4 2025) to $71 (July average) and the high $80s to about $90 by October.

**Spot vs reported averages.** CARB, Oregon and Washington publish the average price of credit transfers, which includes deals priced weeks or months earlier. In a rising market that average sits below spot: in late November 2026 the CARB weekly average was in the low $80s while spot traded near $97 (industry estimates). The desk model uses the published average, so its LCFS value lags a fast move.

**What to watch.** CARB monthly transfer reports and weekly averages; quarterly credit/deficit data; credit bank; AAM trigger test; CARB pathway decisions; litigation.

**Rule of thumb.** $/gal = price × (CI_std − CI_fuel) × MJ/gal ÷ 1,000,000. At $87 and the 2026 standard of 80.17: soy RD (CI 57) ≈ 26¢/gal; UCO RD (CI 20) ≈ 68¢/gal.

## 26. Other state programs (OR, WA, NM) and Canada CFR

**What it is.** Oregon's Clean Fuels Program, Washington's Clean Fuel Standard and New Mexico's Clean Transportation Fuel Program (started 2026) are LCFS-style CI markets with their own targets. Canada's Clean Fuel Regulations set national CI cuts for liquid fuel suppliers.

**How it moves prices.** Together they add a second premium market for low-CI diesel that competes with California for RD barrels. Washington's 2026 step from 3% to 8% and its 45% 2038 target make it the fastest tightener; credits were about $78–83 in October 2026, with Oregon at about $81–86. Canada imports much of its low-CI fuel from the US. Its proposed domestic bonus multipliers (RD 1.5×, BD 1.4×, SAF 1.8×) would favor Canadian production, including canola-based RD, and could reduce US export pull.

**What to watch.** Oregon DEQ and Washington Ecology quarterly data; New Mexico credit pricing; Canada Gazette final CFR amendments; Canadian RD project news.

**Rule of thumb.** Northwest credits usually trade within ±$10 of California, so a barrel's state choice follows the credit-times-CI-gap, net of freight.

## 27. Imported feedstock: the half-RIN rule and tariffs

**What it is.** EPA proposed (June 2025) to halve RINs for imported renewable fuel and for fuel made from foreign feedstock. The final Set 2 rule deferred this to 2028 or later. Separately, 45Z excludes non-North American feedstock from 2026. Tariffs and China's 2024 removal of its UCO export rebate add trade frictions.

**How it moves prices.** Every policy that penalises imported feedstock raises demand for US-origin soybean oil, DCO and domestic fats, and lowers prices for foreign UCO, tallow and canola oil. The 2026 data already show it: Chinese UCO to the US down sharply and Brazilian tallow down, with flows redirected to Europe. If the half-RIN rule arrives in 2028 without a Canada or Mexico exemption, imported canola oil and Canadian RD lose about half their RIN value, a large bullish shock for US soybean oil.

**What to watch.** EPA Set 3 proposal (2028+); Census import data by origin; tariff actions (the Supreme Court ended IEEPA tariffs in February 2026); 45Z final rules.

**Rule of thumb.** Half a D4 RIN at $2 is $1.50/gal for biodiesel (1.5 EV), or about 20¢/lb of feedstock value. That would price most imported oil out of US RFS compliance.

## 28. Biodiesel / renewable diesel producer margin: the full stack

**What it is.** The margin on a gallon is the sum of what the gallon earns minus what it costs:

- **Revenue** = fuel value (ULSD, or the actual B100/RD differential) + RINs (price × EV) + LCFS (or other state credit) + 45Z + by-products (glycerin for FAME; naphtha and LPG for RD).
- **Cost** = feedstock + processing opex (chemicals, energy, hydrogen for RD, labour, maintenance).

**Formulas.**

- Feedstock $/gal = price (¢/lb) × lb/gal ÷ 100. Use 7.5–7.6 lb/gal for FAME and 8.1–8.5 for RD.
- RIN $/gal = D4 price × EV. EV is 1.5 for BD; for RD, 1.7 in 2026 and 1.5 from 2027.
- LCFS $/gal = credit price × (CI_standard − CI_fuel) × energy density × EER ÷ 1,000,000.
  - CI in gCO2e/MJ.
  - Energy density: about 126.1 MJ/gal for BD, 129.65 for RD (ULSD 134.47).
  - EER = 1.0 for diesel substitutes.
  - 2026 California diesel CI standard = 80.17.
- 45Z $/gal = max credit × (50 − CI) ÷ 50, with CI in kg/mmBtu.
- Margin = ULSD + RIN + LCFS + 45Z + by-product − feedstock − opex.
- Opex assumption: $0.40–0.70/gal. It is lower for large FAME plants and higher for RD because of hydrogen and pretreatment. This is an industry rule-of-thumb range, not a published plant cost.

**Worked example (18 Nov 2026 screen levels: ZL 60.0¢, HO $3.94, D4 $2.00, LCFS $87).**

| Line | Soy biodiesel (FAME) | Soy renewable diesel (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Feedstock | 60.0 × 7.55 ÷ 100 = **−$4.53** | 60.0 × 8.3 ÷ 100 = **−$4.98** |
| ULSD (HO) | +$3.94 | +$3.94 |
| RIN | 2.00 × 1.5 = **+$3.00** | 2.00 × 1.7 = **+$3.40** |
| LCFS (CI 55 BD / 57 RD) | 87 × (80.17−55) × 126.13 ÷ 10⁶ = **+$0.28** | 87 × (80.17−57) × 129.65 ÷ 10⁶ = **+$0.26** |
| 45Z (CI 27) | 1.00 × (50−27) ÷ 50 = **+$0.46** | **+$0.46** |
| By-product | glycerin +$0.05 | naphtha/LPG +$0.10 |
| Opex (assumed) | −$0.55 | −$0.65 |
| **Screen margin** | **≈ +$2.65/gal** | **≈ +$2.53/gal** |

**How to read it.** The screen margin is an upper bound. Real plants sell B100 at a discount to HO futures (−$0.95 or worse on some late-2026 forward sales). Not every gallon reaches California, so LCFS may be zero or come from Oregon or Washington instead. 45Z requires prevailing-wage compliance. Applying a −$0.95 physical discount and no LCFS cuts soy FAME to about $1.40/gal. In 2027 RD loses 0.2 RIN (−$0.40 at $2 D4). The breakeven D4 for the marginal gallon = (feedstock + opex − fuel value − LCFS − 45Z − by-product) ÷ EV. That marginal gallon is what anchors the RIN price.

**What to watch.** ZL, HO, D4, LCFS and physical differentials daily; 45Z rules; EV change in 2027.

**Rule of thumb.** At FAME yields, 1¢/lb on oil = 7.5¢/gal of margin, $0.10 on HO = $0.10/gal, and $0.10 on D4 = $0.15/gal.

## 29. EIA feedstock and capacity data

**What it is.** EIA publishes monthly fats, oils and grains consumed for biofuels (million lb: soybean, canola, corn oil, yellow grease/UCO, tallow, white grease, poultry fat), monthly operable capacity, and an annual plant capacity report (at 1 January 2026: biodiesel 128 kb/d, RD and other 324 kb/d). The STEO forecasts production. The old monthly capacity and feedstocks report was folded into EIA's petroleum data tables.

**How it moves prices.** This is the best hard read on feedstock mix: who is winning the slot in biofuel plants. A rising soybean oil share (about 43% in July 2026, up from 33% in 2025) confirms policy is pulling soy. Rising tallow or UCO share means waste fats are winning on CI. Capacity minus production gives utilization, which tells you whether supply can respond to higher RINs.

**What to watch.** EIA monthly feedstock and capacity tables (end of month, about a two-month lag); STEO (around the 10th; the November 2026 STEO cut the 2027 RD forecast); EIA weekly distillate data.

**Rule of thumb.** Divide soybean oil used by about 7.5–8.3 lb/gal to estimate soy-based gallons. Biodiesel near 90% utilization means little spare FAME capacity, so RINs must stay high to call on idle RD.

## 30. EPA EMTS RIN generation data

**What it is.** EPA's EPA Moderated Transaction System publishes monthly RIN generation by D-code and fuel type (domestic BD, RD, imports, renewable jet), separations and retirements, about mid-month for the prior month. EPA also posts weekly volume-weighted RIN prices.

**How it moves prices.** EMTS is the scoreboard against the RVO. D4 generation running ahead of the requirement pace is bearish for RINs; running behind is bullish. In 2026, through September, net D4 supply was only about 64% of the year's BBD need. September generation was about 788 M RINs, but the October release still triggered a sell-off because production surprised high. Import lines show how foreign supply reacts to policy.

**What to watch.** Monthly EMTS release; D4 by fuel type; renewable jet line; available RIN carryover; D6 against conventional need.

**Rule of thumb.** The required monthly pace = (annual requirement − year-to-date net supply) ÷ months left. In Q4 2026 that was about 1.05 B D4/month against an output run-rate of about 0.75–0.8 B, with the difference covered by carryover.

## 31. WASDE soybean oil biofuel use line

**What it is.** In USDA's monthly WASDE, the US soybean oil balance sheet splits domestic use into food, feed and other, and **biofuel** (methyl ester plus renewable diesel and SAF). USDA bases it on EPA rules, EIA data and its own outlook.

**How it moves prices.** It is the official translation of RFS policy into soybean oil demand. Revisions move ZL, and it is the number the trade uses to judge whether the crush can supply the mandate. For 2026/27 USDA forecasts **17.8 B lb** for biofuel, up about 20% y/y and more than half of domestic use. With exports cut to about 0.4 B lb, ending stocks are tight. A higher biofuel line without a higher crush means lower stocks or more imports.

**What to watch.** WASDE (around the 10th–12th); ERS Oil Crops Outlook; NOPA and USDA crush; EIA soybean oil use (compare the pace with USDA's line).

**Rule of thumb.** 17.8 B lb ÷ ~8 lb/gal ≈ 2.2 B gal of soy-based BBD, about 40% of the 2026 BBD mandate. Every 1 B lb revision ≈ 125 M gal, or about 0.2 B D4 RINs.
